# Bayesian Inference and Decision Audits for Public Archives of Frontier AI Evaluations

- **Authors:** Yanan Long
- **Published:** 2026-06-15
- **Categories:** cs.AI, stat.ME
- **arXiv:** [http://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17005v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17005v1)

## Abstract

Public AI evaluations are often read as terminal leaderboards, yet the underlying evidence is a selective time series shaped by reporting rules, benchmark revisions, and missingness. Repeated public archives for LiveBench and Open LLM Leaderboard v2 serve as the primary longitudinal record; LMArena provides a preference stress test; and GAIA and tau-bench contribute limited agentic pilots. Together, these archives instantiate a Bayesian inference problem: under a fixed reporting convention, one constructed terminal-only example over $1{,}000$ systems is compatible with two pre-terminal histories, yielding times of $23.03$ or $75.13$ to reach within $0.05$ of the ceiling under the same terminal-tail model. In synthetic posterior comparisons, action-facing diagnostics differ across observation regimes. The candidate selection-aware frontier model fails synthetic recovery, objective-archive prediction, preference transfer, and uncertainty calibration; correspondingly, fixed audit gates reject its stronger claims. An archive-and-adjudication protocol reconstructs public evaluation histories, isolates a verified timing boundary, and falsifies unsupported frontier claims.

## Research content

The paper develops a Bayesian inference framework and decision audit methodology for public archives of frontier AI evaluations. It analyzes repeated public archives including LiveBench, Open LLM Leaderboard v2, LMArena, GAIA, and tau-bench as longitudinal records. The authors demonstrate that under fixed reporting conventions, a single terminal-only observation can be compatible with multiple pre-terminal histories, leading to substantially different times to reach near-ceiling performance. They evaluate a candidate selection-aware frontier model against synthetic recovery, objective-archive prediction, preference transfer, and uncertainty calibration, finding it fails on all dimensions. The paper proposes an archive-and-adjudication protocol to reconstruct evaluation histories, establish verified timing boundaries, and falsify unsupported claims.

## Problem addressed

Public AI evaluations are typically presented as terminal leaderboards, but the underlying evidence consists of selective time series shaped by reporting rules, benchmark revisions, and missing data. This creates challenges for interpreting the true trajectory of AI system capabilities and for validating claims about frontier performance.
